The two numbers stamped on a steel bolt head are a compact strength specification. They come from ISO 898-1, which sets the mechanical properties of bolts, screws and studs made of carbon steel and alloy steel. The current edition is ISO 898-1:2013, the fifth, with a technical corrigendum issued the same year; ISO confirmed it in 2025 and a revision is under way.

## Reading the two numbers

Take **8.8**:

- The **first number × 100** is the nominal tensile strength in MPa: 8 → 800 MPa.
- The **second number** is ten times the ratio of nominal yield strength to nominal tensile strength: .8 → 0.8.
- So first × second × 10 gives the nominal yield strength: 8 × 8 × 10 = 640 MPa.

In the same way 10.9 means 1000 MPa and 900 MPa, and 12.9 means 1200 MPa and 1080 MPa.

<Note kind="warn" title="Design with the minimum values, not the nominal ones">
The nominal values exist only for the designation system. The standard specifies **minimum** values, and these are what a strength calculation must use. For 10.9, for example, the minimum tensile strength is 1040 MPa and the minimum yield 940 MPa, both higher than the nominal 1000 and 900.
</Note>

| Class | Rm nominal (MPa) | Rm min (MPa) | Yield min (MPa) | Proof stress Sp (MPa) | Elongation A min (%) | Hardness (HV) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.6 | 400 | 400 | ReL 240 | 225 | 22 | 120–220 |
| 4.8 | 400 | 420 | Rpf 340 | 310 | — | 130–220 |
| 5.6 | 500 | 500 | ReL 300 | 280 | 20 | 155–220 |
| 5.8 | 500 | 520 | Rpf 420 | 380 | — | 160–220 |
| 6.8 | 600 | 600 | Rpf 480 | 440 | — | 190–250 |
| 8.8, d ≤ 16 mm | 800 | 800 | Rp0.2 640 | 580 | 12 | 250–320 |
| 8.8, d > 16 mm | 800 | 830 | Rp0.2 660 | 600 | 12 | 255–335 |
| 9.8, d ≤ 16 mm only | 900 | 900 | Rp0.2 720 | 650 | 10 | 290–360 |
| 10.9 | 1000 | 1040 | Rp0.2 940 | 830 | 9 | 320–380 |
| 12.9 | 1200 | 1220 | Rp0.2 1100 | 970 | 8 | 385–435 |

Notes on the table:

- For 4.8, 5.8 and 6.8 the standard marks the Rpf values as under investigation and gives them only to calculate the proof-stress ratio. Instead of A, measured on a machined test piece, it specifies the elongation of the full-size fastener Af: 0.24, 0.22 and 0.20.
- 8.8 has two rows: above 16 mm the standard sets slightly higher minimum values. 9.8 is defined only up to 16 mm.
- The **proof load** of a bolt is Sp × A<sub>s</sub>, where A<sub>s</sub> is the stressed area of the thread. The bolt must carry it without permanent elongation. For an M10 coarse thread, A<sub>s</sub> = 58.0 mm², so an 8.8 bolt has a proof load of 580 × 58.0 ≈ 33.6 kN and a minimum tensile load of 800 × 58.0 ≈ 46.4 kN.

## Two routes to 12.9

ISO 898-1 allows class 12.9 to be made in two ways, with the same mechanical properties; its tables list the class twice, once with the designation underlined. One route is alloy steel, quenched and tempered at 425 °C or more. The other is carbon steel with additives such as boron, manganese, chromium or molybdenum, quenched and tempered at 380 °C or more.

The standard also advises caution when class 12.9 is considered: the manufacturer's capability, the service conditions and the tightening method should be taken into account, and the environment can cause stress corrosion cracking, in both uncoated and coated fasteners.

## Marking

- Hexagon and hexalobular head bolts and screws with a thread diameter of 5 mm or more must carry the **manufacturer's identification mark and the property class**, for all classes.
- The dot may be left out: "88" and "109" are valid markings.
- A leading zero, such as **08.8** or **010.9**, marks a fastener with reduced loadability: it may not carry the full loads specified for its class.

## Matching nuts

Nuts follow ISO 898-2, whose current edition is ISO 898-2:2022. Its property classes are 04, 05, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 12; class 9 has been deleted. A nut's class number is the first number of the strongest bolt class it is designed to carry:

| Bolt | Nut |
|---|---|
| 5.8 | 5 |
| 6.8 | 6 |
| 8.8 | 8 |
| 10.9 | 10 |
| 12.9 | 12 |

A nut of a higher class may replace one of a lower class.

## Stainless steel bolts

Stainless fasteners follow ISO 3506-1, current edition 2020, and are marked differently: steel group and grade, a dash, then the property class.

- The letter gives the structure: **A** austenitic, **C** martensitic, **F** ferritic, **D** duplex. The digit after it is the alloy type within the group.
- The two digits after the dash are one tenth of the minimum tensile strength.

| Marking | Meaning | Rm min (MPa) | Rp0.2 min (MPa) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A2-70 | Austenitic (1.4301 type, no Mo), cold worked | 700 | 450 |
| A4-80 | Austenitic with 2–3 % Mo (1.4401 / 1.4404 type), "acid-resistant" | 800 | 600 |
| A2-50, A4-50 | Austenitic, class 50 | 500 | 210 |

An A2-70 bolt is therefore weaker than an 8.8 steel bolt, and its yield is a smaller share of its tensile strength (450/700 ≈ 0.64, against 640/800 = 0.8). Do not swap one for the other without recalculating the joint.
