7075 is a high-strength aluminium–zinc–magnesium–copper alloy of the 7xxx series. Its typical T6 tensile strength, 572 MPa, is nearly twice that of 6061-T6. It is made into aircraft structures, highly stressed parts, and moulds, jigs and fixtures. Three facts shape how it is bought and machined: it is not arc welded, T6 has low resistance to stress corrosion cracking, which the T73 temper was developed to overcome, and thick plate keeps quench stresses that can distort a machined part.

## Heat treatment

| Step | Conditions |
|---|---|
| Solution treatment | 460–499 °C for sheet, plate and rolled bar; 460–471 °C for extrusions (MIL-H-6088G). Stockists give 470–480 °C. |
| Quench | Water; the bath at no more than 38 °C at the end of the quench (MIL-H-6088G) |
| Ageing to T6 | About 120 °C for 24 h |
| Ageing to T73 | Two steps: 105–107 °C for 6–8 h, then 163–177 °C. Second step about 24–30 h at 165 °C for plate, 8–10 h at 175 °C for bar and forgings, 6–8 h at 175 °C for extrusions (Total Materia) |
| Ageing to T76 | 3–30 h at 121 °C, then 15–18 h at 163 °C (Total Materia) |
| Annealing to O | 415 °C for 2–3 h, cool to 205 °C or below, then 4 h at 230 °C (Total Materia). BIKAR: 380–420 °C, cool at 30 °C/h or less to 230 °C, hold 3–5 h |

The T6 practice is the same in Total Materia, Kaiser's NASA report and two US patents. MIL-H-6088G warns that 7075 can begin to melt above 482 °C (900 °F) and recommends no more than 488 °C for plate and bar over 4 in (102 mm); Total Materia gives 490 °C to avoid eutectic melting. Ageing to T73 or T76 needs tighter control of time, temperature and heating rate than T6 (Total Materia).

## Stress corrosion and the conductivity check

ECSS-Q-70-36A, the European space standard for stress-corrosion selection, rates 7075-T6 low, T76 moderate and T73 high, and asks for mechanically stress-relieved products (TX51 and similar) where possible. In its article on stress-corrosion cracking, Total Materia notes that 7079-T6, 7075-T6 and 2024-T3 account for more than 90 % of service failures among high-strength aluminium alloys, and that the limits of 7075-T6 led to the T73 and T76 tempers. US patents on 7xxx ageing agree that T73 lowers strength "significantly" against T6.

Because overageing raises electrical conductivity, conductivity is the routine check that a part really is T73. AMS 2658D gives typical ranges of 30.5–36.0 % IACS for T6 and 40.0–43.0 % IACS for T73; the two do not overlap. NASA's process specification PRC-2002 requires conductivity tests to ASTM E1004 against the AMS 2658 values. One stockist page gives 33 % IACS for T7351, which is a T6 value.

## Quench stresses and distortion

Quenching thick plate leaves compression at the surfaces and tension in the core. Stretching the plate by 1.5–3 % (T651, T7351) cuts these stresses by about a factor of ten, but what is left is still enough to distort aircraft parts machined from the plate; the rework and scrap cost the industry tens of millions of dollars a year. Those measurements were made by Prime and co-workers at Los Alamos on 7050, a related alloy; studies on pre-stretched 7075-T7351 plate measure the residual stress to predict machining distortion.

For machined parts, buy stretched material: T651 or T7351 plate, T6511 or T73511 extrusions. Producers disagree on how stable 7075 plate is in machining: BIKAR and GLEICH rate its dimensional stability poor, Metall Service Menziken good.

## Welding

7075 is not arc welded. The producer sheets rate gas, TIG and MIG welding unsuitable (Metall Service Menziken alone says conditional), and ESAB calls it unweldable by arc welding. For electron-beam welding they disagree: Euralliage rates it good, GLEICH poor. Copper and magnesium together make the weld sensitive to cracking as it solidifies (ESAB, TWI), and The Fabricator notes that the heat-affected zone is prone to cracking and to stress corrosion in service. Resistance spot and seam welding are rated good, and friction stir welding joins 7xxx alloys with low distortion (TWI). Brazing and soldering are not suitable.

## Corrosion and anodizing

General corrosion resistance is poor in the atmosphere and in seawater; MARKS advises protection outdoors. Protective and hard anodizing work; decorative anodizing does not, according to every producer sheet that rates it. Metall Service Menziken adds that hard chrome and electroless nickel plating are possible.

## Machining

7075 belongs to ISO 513 group N. Seco uses AW-7075 as the reference material of its group N1 and recommends its uncoated H25 or coated F15M grades first. ISCAR files hardenable wrought aluminium under group 22 with kc1.1 = 800 N/mm² and mc = 0.25, without naming 7075; that gives about 1200 N/mm² at h = 0.2 mm. No other tool maker's kc value was found.

**Temper.** Machine it aged. MARKS says chips break well only in the aged condition. The producers' ratings disagree: GLEICH, Metall Service Menziken and voestalpine call 7075 very good or excellent to machine, Aircraft Materials and Smiths only fair; MatWeb puts T6 and T73 at 70 % on its aluminium scale. The scales differ, so the ratings are not comparable.

**Speeds.** Wieland gives 300–800 m/min. Haas lists 610 m/min for its HSAM2 carbide end mills in wrought aluminium (ISCAR groups 21–22), with fz 0.025–0.254 mm for diameters of 3–25 mm. Built-up edge and the remedies for it are the same as for [6061 and 6082](/materials/6061-6082-aluminium/).

**Moulds and fixtures.** BIKAR and GLEICH rate 7075 very good for EDM and polishing, which suits mould and model making.

## Choosing a temper

- **T651 plate** for strength: jigs, fixtures, moulds and parts not exposed to stress corrosion.
- **T7351 plate** for parts under sustained stress in a corrosive environment (Online Metals, ECSS), accepting about 10–20 % less strength; the proof strength drops most.
- **Thick sections**: check the minimum for the actual thickness. From 50 mm the minimum proof strength falls steadily, from 440 MPa at 50–60 mm to 260 MPa at 120–150 mm.
- **Arc-welded designs**: not 7075.

Typical uses named in the sources: aircraft structures and fittings; gears and shafts; tool, mould and model making, jigs and fixtures; defence and missile parts; machine frames and robot arms; motorsport parts; bicycle and climbing equipment.
