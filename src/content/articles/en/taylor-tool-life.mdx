Frederick W. Taylor presented the results of years of cutting experiments to the American Society of Mechanical Engineers in December 1906; they were published in 1907 as "On the Art of Cutting Metals". His central finding still sets cutting speeds today: tool life falls steeply, and predictably, as cutting speed rises.

## The equation

$$
V \cdot T^{\,n} = C
$$

- **V** is the cutting speed, in m/min.
- **T** is the tool life, in minutes, until the chosen wear criterion is reached (for example VB = 0.3 mm; see [tool wear](/fundamentals/tool-wear/)).
- **n** is an exponent that depends mainly on the tool material.
- **C** is the cutting speed that gives a tool life of exactly one minute. It depends on the tool, the work material, the feed, the depth of cut and the wear criterion.

Taking logarithms gives log V = log C − n · log T, a straight line on log–log axes with slope −n. A small n means a flat line: a small increase in speed costs a lot of tool life. A large n means the tool tolerates speed better.

## Finding n and C from two tests

Two tests at different speeds, run to the same wear criterion, define the line.

$$
n = \frac{\ln(V_1/V_2)}{\ln(T_2/T_1)} \qquad C = V_1 \cdot T_1^{\,n}
$$

**Example.** At V₁ = 250 m/min the tool reaches VB = 0.3 mm after T₁ = 15 min. At V₂ = 200 m/min it lasts T₂ = 40 min.

- n = ln(250/200) / ln(40/15) = 0.2231 / 0.9808 ≈ 0.228
- C = 250 · 15<sup>0.228</sup> ≈ 463 m/min

Check: 463 / 40<sup>0.228</sup> ≈ 200 m/min. ✓

With these values, a 10 % increase in speed from 200 to 220 m/min shortens tool life from 40 to about 26 minutes: T = (C/V)<sup>1/n</sup>.

## Typical values of n

| Tool material | Kalpakjian & Schmid | ACSCE module | Groover (turning steel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-speed steel | 0.08–0.2 | 0.1–0.18 | 0.125 (C = 70 m/min) |
| Cast cobalt alloys | 0.1–0.15 | — | — |
| Carbides | 0.2–0.5 (all carbides) | 0.2–0.25 uncoated; 0.3 TiC/TiN coated; 0.4 Al₂O₃ coated | 0.25, cemented carbide (C = 500 m/min) |
| Ceramics | 0.5–0.7 | 0.4–0.7 | 0.6 (C = 3000 m/min) |

The sources agree for HSS and ceramics. For carbides, Kalpakjian gives one wide range for all grades; the ACSCE module splits it by coating, and its coated values are the only ones we found. Groover's C values apply to turning steel at f = 0.25 mm/rev and d = 2.5 mm.

## The extended equation

Feed and depth of cut also affect tool life. The extended form adds them with their own exponents:

$$
V \cdot T^{\,n} \cdot f^{\,x} \cdot d^{\,y} = C
$$

x and y are found by experiment, and different textbooks attach the letters to f and d the other way round. As with n and C, the exponents hold only for the tool, work material and range of conditions they were measured in.

## Which tool life to aim for

Running faster removes metal sooner but spends more edges and more time changing them. Two classic optima follow from the Taylor equation.

**Maximum production rate** (shortest time per part), with T<sub>c</sub> the time to change an edge:

$$
T_{\max\,\text{prod}} = \left(\frac{1}{n}-1\right) T_c
$$

**Minimum cost per part**, with C<sub>t</sub> the cost of one cutting edge and C<sub>m</sub> the cost per minute of machine and operator:

$$
T_{\min\,\text{cost}} = \left(\frac{1}{n}-1\right)\left(T_c + \frac{C_t}{C_m}\right)
$$

The matching speed in each case is V = C / T<sup>n</sup>.

**Example.** Carbide with n = 0.25 and C = 500 m/min. Changing an edge takes T<sub>c</sub> = 2 min, one edge costs C<sub>t</sub> = 3 currency units, and the machine with operator costs C<sub>m</sub> = 1 unit per minute.

- T<sub>max prod</sub> = (1/0.25 − 1) · 2 = 6 min → V = 500 / 6<sup>0.25</sup> ≈ 319 m/min
- T<sub>min cost</sub> = 3 · (2 + 3/1) = 15 min → V = 500 / 15<sup>0.25</sup> ≈ 254 m/min

The speed for minimum cost is always lower than the speed for maximum production. The band between them is often called the high-efficiency range: slower than the lower bound wastes machine time, faster than the upper bound wastes tools without gaining output.

<Note kind="key" title="What the formulas leave out">
Both optima assume that only tool life changes with speed. Real limits often come first: spindle power, chip control, surface finish, or a tool-change interval tied to a pallet or a shift. Use the optimum speed as a starting point and check it against those limits.
</Note>
