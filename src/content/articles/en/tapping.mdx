A tap makes an internal thread in a drilled hole. A cutting tap removes the thread grooves as chips; a forming tap presses them into the wall and makes no chips at all. Either way the tap advances exactly one pitch per revolution, so the feed is not a free choice. What you choose is the tap: where it sends the chips, over how many threads its chamfer spreads the cut, which tolerance class it cuts, and how the machine drives it.

The hole comes first, and its size is covered in [tap drill sizes](/processes/tap-drill-sizes/). This page is about the tap and the process.

| Tap | Hole | Chips | Work material |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spiral point (form B) | Through | Pushed ahead, out of the far end | Long-chipping |

Völkel describes the two chip-moving designs plainly: the spiral point "pushes the chips generated during tapping forward in the cutting direction", while helical flutes "convey the chips back towards the tool shank and the opening of the tap drill hole". With a straight-fluted form C tap, in Völkel's words, "the chips are not actively conveyed out of the tap drill hole". Seco's selection guide sorts them by chip type: for long-chipping materials such as steel, stainless steel and aluminium, a spiral point for through holes and spiral flutes for blind holes; for short-chipping cast iron, straight flutes. LUBERO's form descriptions point the same way: form B, with its spiral point (a "peel cut"), is for through holes in long-chipping materials.

A spiral point does not belong in a blind hole. Haas warns that the chips can pack into the bottom of the hole until the tap bottoms out. Seco adds the opposite case: if there is little space behind a through hole, pull the chips back with spiral flutes even there.

**Helix angle.** Right-hand spiral-flute taps are made with different helix angles. Völkel lists 15°, 35°, 39° and 40°, Dormer's Shark Line runs from 15° to 48°, ISCAR's speed tables have a column for R40° taps, and YG-1 marks its Prime spiral-flute taps R40 and its synchro tap R45. Seco notes that high-tensile materials may need a smaller helix angle.

**Left-hand flutes for through holes.** On a right-hand tap, left-hand spiral flutes push the chips forward, as a spiral point does. ISCAR lists the left-hand spiral-fluted tap under through holes, and CERATIZIT's TruTap DL has 15° left-hand flutes that send the chips in the cutting direction.

### Forming taps

A forming tap has no cutting edges. Lobes on its thread press the material outward and let it flow into the crests of the thread, so there are no chips to clear: DC Swiss sells one tool for both through and blind holes. The thread is stronger because, as Völkel puts it, "the grain structure is deformed, not separated"; DC Swiss promises higher resistance to stripping. The price is a narrower window:

- **Ductile material only.** ISCAR, DC Swiss and Sutton ask for at least 10 % elongation; YG-1 and the code key in ISCAR's own guide accept 8 to 10 %. Völkel: "Very hard or brittle materials lead to cracks, dimensional problems, or tool damage."
- **Strength limits depend on the tool.** DC Swiss gives up to 1,150 N/mm², CERATIZIT up to 1,400 N/mm² for its DuoForm formers, and Völkel about 700 N/mm² for steel with its VAP finish and 900 N/mm² with TiN.
- **A larger hole**, roughly d − 0.45 P to d − 0.5 P: Völkel drills 7.45 mm for M8, against 6.8 mm for a cutting tap. The values per maker are in [tap drill sizes](/processes/tap-drill-sizes/).
- **More torque:** "about 40–50% more torque than cutting" (Völkel).
- **Lubrication without gaps.** DC Swiss warns that if the supply of lubricant is interrupted, cold welding quickly destroys the tool. None of the sources requires neat oil: YG-1's forming test ran on a 9 % water-soluble emulsion.

## Chamfer forms

The chamfer (lead) is the tapered start of the tap. Its first threads are ground down to a cone, so each tooth cuts only part of the thread depth and the full profile builds up over several teeth. DIN 2197 names the chamfer forms with letters; tap makers print them as follows.

The ranges are not quite uniform; the values in brackets are the second reading. For form B, ISCAR, Dormer, DC Swiss and CERATIZIT's product pages print 3.5–5 threads, while YG-1, Völkel and the definitions page of the same CERATIZIT catalogue print 4–5. Form D shows the same split within CERATIZIT's catalogue. Form F appears only in ISCAR's table: LUBERO describes five standard forms, A to E. ISCAR advises avoiding the very short forms where possible. The standard itself was not available to us, so the table gives the values as the makers print them.

**Why the chamfer length matters.** LUBERO estimates the chip each tooth cuts from the pitch P, the number of flutes z and the chamfer length l₄:

$$
a_e = \frac{0.5 \cdot P^2}{z \cdot l_4}
$$

For an M10 × 1.5 tap with three flutes, form C (2 to 3 threads, l₄ = 3 to 4.5 mm) gives 0.08 to 0.13 mm per tooth; form B (3.5 to 5 threads, l₄ = 5.25 to 7.5 mm) gives 0.05 to 0.07 mm. A long chamfer is gentler on each tooth, but in a blind hole it costs depth: the chamfer threads leave incomplete threads at the bottom, so the hole has to reach beyond the full thread by the chamfer length plus some clearance. Haas asks for blind holes drilled at least 0.050 in (1.3 mm) deeper than the tap is programmed to go.

## Tolerance classes

The class marked on a tap does not describe the tap's own tolerance: it names the nut-thread classes the tap is meant to cut. ISO 2857 and DIN EN 22857 call these application classes ISO 1, 2 and 3; the old DIN 802 marking used the nut class instead.

| Tap class | DIN 802 (old) | Nut classes it cuts |
|---|---|---|
| ISO 1 | 4H | 4H, 5H |
| ISO 2 | 6H | 4G, 5G, 6H |
| ISO 3 | 6G | 6G, 7H, 8H |
| – | 7G | 7G, 8G |

ISO 2 (6H) is the default: Vergnano calls it the standard fit, and Völkel and Seco call 6H the standard and most common class for metric threads. ISO 1 (4H) is for precise fits. ISO 3 (6G) and 7G are for loose fits and for parts that are coated or plated after tapping, which need the extra room; for hot-dip galvanizing Völkel offers taps 0.1 mm oversize.

**The X classes.** Tap makers also sell 4HX, 6HX, 6GX and 7GX. Vergnano places them between the standard classes, 6HX between 6H and 6G and 6GX between 6G and 7G, and gives two reasons for them:

- **Abrasive materials** such as cast iron and aluminium-silicon alloys, where a slightly larger tap lasts longer. Seco also raises the class to 6HX for abrasive materials.
- **Forming taps**, because the formed material springs back elastically: to get a 6H thread, Vergnano says, you need a 6HX forming tap.

The X classes are not part of DIN EN 22857. ISCAR notes that the codes for 7G/8G and the X zones are yet to be standardized there, so the values from DIN 802 remain valid.

The spindle speed follows from the cutting speed and the nominal diameter:

$$
n = \frac{1000 \cdot v_c}{\pi \cdot d}
$$

M10 at 10 m/min turns at 318 rpm.

## Feed: one pitch per revolution

Each revolution, the tap screws itself one pitch deeper, so the feed per revolution is the pitch, and the feed rate is

$$
v_f = P \cdot n
$$

Haas's example for an M12 × 1.75 tap at 500 rpm: F = 1.75 × 500 = 875 mm/min. For inch taps the feed rate is the speed divided by the threads per inch: a 13 TPI tap at 500 rpm needs 500 / 13 = 38.4615 in/min. On machines that program feed per revolution, the feed is simply the pitch.

## Rigid or floating

There are two ways to keep the feed in step with the spindle.

**Rigid (synchronous) tapping.** The control positions the spindle like an axis and moves the feed axis in step with it, with a tap held solidly in the spindle. It needs a machine with a position-controlled spindle: Siemens allows CYCLE84 only if the spindle "is technically able to go into position-controlled spindle operation", and Haas requires its rigid tapping option to tap without a floating holder.

**Tapping with a compensating (floating) holder.** On a machine that cannot synchronise, the holder lets the tap move axially, so the tap's own thread takes over the lead and absorbs the difference between the programmed feed and the true pitch (BILZ). Smid's objection: floating holders make it difficult to hold an exact thread depth, particularly in blind holes.

| Control | Rigid tapping | With a compensating holder |
|---|---|---|
| FANUC | M29 S… before G84 (right-hand) or G74 (left-hand) | G84, G74 |
| Siemens SINUMERIK | CYCLE84, "tapping without compensating chuck" | CYCLE840, "tapping with compensating chuck" |
| HEIDENHAIN TNC | Cycle 207, rigid tapping | Cycle 206, with a floating tap holder |
| Haas | G84 / G74 with the rigid tapping option | G84 / G74 with a floating holder |

HEIDENHAIN adds Cycle 209, tapping with chip breaking. G84 is for right-hand taps and G74 for left-hand taps.

**Feed with a compensating holder.** Several sources program a little less than the full lead, so the holder works in tension instead of pushing the tap. Sutton recommends 95 % for spiral-flute taps, "to eliminate tap cutting oversize": M6 × 1 at 1,000 rpm becomes 950 mm/min. For tension-compression holders, Haas says to use 90 % of the calculated feed "to allow constant pressure on the tap as it enters and exits the hole". Allen Krenick of T.M. Smith Tool wrote in 1996 that some users of length-compensating holders on machining centres use only the tension feature and program 98 % of the pitch, especially when they have to program the feed per minute. These are three practices for different holders, not one rule.

**Synchro holders.** Even rigid tapping is not perfectly synchronous. Holders with a small built-in compensation absorb the remaining errors and the pitch tolerance of the tap; BILZ, DC Swiss, YG-1 and CERATIZIT claim longer tap life and better threads with them. BILZ gives its holder ±0.5 mm of spring compensation. In one OSG test, the same M6 spiral-flute tap made 2,798 holes in S45C carbon steel at 15 m/min with synchronised feed, and 1,726 holes in a floating holder.

Haas's own checklist for rigid tapping: start from an R plane at least 5 mm (0.200 in) above the work so the spindle can synchronise and accelerate; hold the tap in a positive drive; and keep the runout low, as some taps break if the runout exceeds 0.03 mm (0.001 in).

## Torque and power

LUBERO, a German tap maker, and SUHNER, a maker of tapping units and spindles, print the same estimate for the torque of a cutting tap:

$$
M_d = \frac{k_c \cdot P^2 \cdot d}{8000} \quad [\text{N·m}]
$$

with the specific cutting force kc in N/mm², and the pitch P and nominal diameter d in mm. The cutting power follows from the torque and speed. Both sources divide by 9,500; the exact constant for kW, N·m and rpm is 60,000 / 2π = 9,549, which gives values 0.5 % lower. The calculator uses 9,549.

$$
P_c = \frac{M_d \cdot n}{9549} \quad [\text{kW}]
$$

The pitch enters squared, so torque climbs steeply with size: M10 × 1.5 needs about 30 times the torque of M3 × 0.5. In unalloyed structural steel (kc = 2,300 N/mm²), M10 × 1.5 takes 2300 × 1.5² × 10 / 8000 = 6.5 N·m; at 10 m/min, or 318 rpm, that is 0.22 kW at the tap, before the losses of the machine.

|---|---|---|
| Unalloyed structural steel | ≤ 700 | 2,300 |
| Free-cutting steel | ≤ 700 | 2,300 |
| Structural steel | 500–950 | 2,500 |
| Quenched and tempered steel, medium strength | 500–950 | 2,600 |
| Nitriding steel, quenched and tempered | 950–1,400 | 3,600 |
| Tool steel | 950–1,400 | 3,600 |
| Austenitic stainless and acid-resistant steel | 500–950 | 3,200 |

## Why taps break

A tap is slender, weakened by its flutes and locked in its own thread. The troubleshooting tables of the tap makers and of Haas name the same causes again and again:

- **The tap hits the bottom of the hole.** Drill blind holes deeper; Haas asks for at least 0.050 in (1.3 mm) beyond the programmed depth. ISCAR recommends a holder with axial float and a slipping clutch.
- **The chips pack.** A spiral-point tap in a blind hole is the classic case (Haas). Tungaloy points out that a tap cuts continuously and makes a large amount of chips that can clog it; YG-1's remedy for chip packing is a tap that moves the chips, or a forming tap.
- **The hole is too small.** ISCAR lists an undersized pre-hole as a cause of breakage, and YG-1 answers excessive torque with a larger drill. Yamawa reports that opening a hole from 6.8 to 6.9 mm cut the cutting volume by 15 %.
- **Misalignment and runout.** The axes of the tap and the hole must line up (ISCAR); Haas has seen taps break at more than 0.03 mm runout.
- **The wrong cycle.** Seco warns that choosing the wrong tapping cycle on a CNC machine breaks taps, because the feed and the spindle rotation are then not synchronised.
- **Too deep a thread.** Haas: the risk rises sharply beyond one and a half times the major diameter.
- **Hard materials and small taps.** Tungaloy notes that the load on the tap rises in hard materials, and that small-diameter taps are especially prone to breaking.
- **Blunt taps and weak lubrication.** ISCAR lists a blunt tap as a cause of breakage, and warns that the lubricant content of a general machining coolant is often too low for tapping.
- **The wrong tap.** Hand taps are not designed for CNC machines (Haas).
