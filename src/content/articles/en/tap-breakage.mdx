Taps are prone to breaking under high loads, Tungaloy notes, narrow ones especially, and getting a broken tap out of a part is difficult and can damage the part. Haas's troubleshooting guide and the tables of ISCAR and YG-1 name the same causes again and again. Here they are in the order that is quickest to check at the machine; the general background is in [tapping](/processes/tapping/).

## How deep to drill a blind hole

One of the commonest causes of breakage needs only arithmetic. To get a full thread to depth L, the tap's tip has to go deeper by the length of its chamfer, which only cuts incomplete threads; the drilled hole has to go deeper still, and the drill point adds a cone below that. Haas also reminds programmers that the tip angle makes a hole shallower than the programmed depth if it is not allowed for.

For an M10 × 1.5 thread 15 mm deep with a form C tap (2–3 chamfer threads), the tap goes to 15 + 3 × 1.5 = 19.5 mm, the drill at full diameter to at least 20.8 mm and the drill point to 23.3 mm. A form B tap, with 3.5–5 chamfer threads (4–5 at YG-1), needs up to 3 mm more. That extra depth is why blind holes with little room below the thread take the short chamfers C and E, although ISCAR advises avoiding E where possible and Seco counts using it where a longer chamfer would fit as a common error that shortens tool life. Form B is a through-hole tap in any case: its spiral point pushes the chips ahead (Haas).

## Other tapping faults

ISCAR's and YG-1's tables pair each fault with its usual causes:

| Fault | Likely causes | Remedies |
|---|---|---|
| Oversize thread | Wrong tap for the material; misalignment; the tap jams; chips pack; galling; a tap reground off centre | A tap for the material group; align; better lubrication and a corrected speed; spiral flutes or a spiral point; regrind concentric |
| Undersize thread | Tap limits too small (YG-1) | An oversize tap; YG-1 also corrects the chamfer and the cutting angle |
| Bell-mouthed entry | Too little or wrong starting pressure | More starting pressure; a holder with axial float |
| Stripped thread | Wrong geometry; spindle and feed out of step | A tap for the material; check the feed, axial float |
| Rough or torn thread | A blunt or badly reground tap; a chamfer too short | Regrind or replace; a longer chamfer (YG-1) |
| Chipped teeth | Poor lubrication; chips jamming; the tap hits the bottom; too short a lead after regrinding | More and better coolant; a different tap or speed; a deeper hole; keep the lead when regrinding |
| Rapid wear | Irregular material; wrong speed; lean coolant; a work-hardened pre-hole | Adjust speed and coolant; drill the pre-hole carefully; a coated or vanadium HSS tap (YG-1) |
| Galling | Material welding to the tap | A thread-relieved tap, a surface treatment, proper lubricant, lower speed, a larger hole (YG-1) |

## Forming taps fail differently

A forming tap does not jam on chips, but it is sensitive to the hole and the lubrication. DC Swiss warns that a hole that is too small gives too big a profile and needs more torque, and that if the lubricant supply is interrupted, cold welding quickly ends the tool's life. Dormer gives the correction from the thread itself: if material is pushed up at the entry, choose a slightly larger drill; if the profile is not filled, a slightly smaller one. Völkel adds that forming takes about 40–50 % more torque than cutting, so the machine needs that reserve.

## When to change the method

Where taps keep breaking, Tungaloy suggests a thread mill: it cuts intermittently with a low and steady cutting force, makes short broken chips, and if it does break, only its fragments have to be removed. The price is programming, since a thread mill follows a helical path (G02/G03 with Z) instead of a fixed tapping cycle. For small threads, Haas recommends forming taps, which are stronger than cutting taps.
